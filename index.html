Fork Commons is public software, held in trust

Open-source the world. All of it.

Wall Street has learned to buy the software that runs our towns and charge rent on systems we can't afford to leave. We're building the way out: free, open-source software your town, newsroom or station owns outright.

The rent always comes due

Private equity has spent years buying the companies behind city agendas, permits, public records, municipal codes and school systems. The customers are perfect: a city can't stop issuing permits, and leaving a system it has run for fifteen years can cost more than staying.

So prices climb, features become add-ons, and the public's records sit on a server the public doesn't control.

The monthly charge was just the visible part. The real transaction was ownership.Scott Converse, Open-Source the World. All of It.

Longmont already did this once

When the telecom companies said it couldn't be done, Longmont built NextLight, its own city-owned fiber network. In 2026, PCMag's readers named it the top internet provider in America, ahead of every national carrier.

If a town can own the network that carries its information, it can own the software that runs on it.

Source: Broadband Breakfast, June 2, 2026

Your AI, on your own machine

Nearly everything here runs on AI models on your own computer, through Ollama, LM Studio or llama.cpp, with Whisper for speech. Cloud models are a choice you make, never a requirement, and TownLight's core workflows run with no AI at all.

34 products run on local models today. Look for the chip Local AI on every program page. The next step is the town, then the region.

Planned: in design now

Next: AI the community owns

The civic tools here lean on AI. The next step is making sure that intelligence belongs to the community too, so a town that leaves one landlord doesn't move in with another.

The plan: own the core, rent the overflow, keep the switch. Openly licensed models run on machines the community owns, connected over Longmont's own fiber. One gateway meters every request, enforces the cooperative's rules, and can send overflow to whichever host is serving the same open models best that week.

  • Shared machines get more done. Serving many people at once lets one machine produce many times the output it would for a single user. That's the math that makes a town-sized utility work.
  • Open models are close behind. Independent tracking puts the best open models a matter of months behind the best closed ones, plenty for summarizing a meeting, searching records or drafting a grant letter.
  • Sensitive records stay home. City work runs on separate, locked-down hardware that never touches the public service.
  • Library card sign-in. Residents get an allowance, and the civic apps here become the first users on day one.
  • Neighbors can share capacity. Members can lend spare machines overnight, and town utilities can link up, settling accounts the way libraries handle interlibrary loans.

How one person builds this much

Fork Commons is built by one developer directing teams of AI coding agents, with a rule that sits under everything: the agent writes claims, never verdicts. Every claim has to point at evidence that a separate tool checks.

Those tools are open source too. Use them on your own projects.

Visit the workshop: 22 tools

$ verdict
  Evidence-first verification for AI coding agents. The agent writes claims, never verdicts.
$ verdictlite
  Makes the coding agent run the checks before it stops.
$ rigwell
  A local workbench for coding agents: workstreams, missions, review and pull requests.
$ devharmonics
  A local, host-agnostic software factory built to survive any one vendor disappearing.
$ prover
  Checks exactly which bytes and claims a release actually contains.
$ installer-ci
  Tests real installs end to end across 13 installer types, built never to false-pass.
$ tampercheck
  Reports whether a change weakened the project's own tests.

Declaration of trust

What Fork Commons promises everyone who depends on its software.

  1. Free, and kept freeEvery product ships under an open-source license. Nobody can close it later.
  2. Your data leaves with youRecords stay in formats you can export, read and take anywhere.
  3. Run it yourself, or have it hostedYour data and your setup stay yours either way.
  4. Public money builds public assetsWhat a town pays to improve goes back into the commons for every town.
  5. Honest about statusEvery product says plainly whether it's live, in beta or still being built.
  6. Held in trustA nonprofit holds the code and the name, so it can't be sold out from under you.

Take it. Improve it. Give it back.

Download a product, point it at your town, and tell us what breaks. When it works, give it away. That's how you open-source the world.
