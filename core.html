Next: AI the community owns

The civic tools here lean on AI. The next step is making sure that intelligence belongs to the community too, so a town that leaves one landlord doesn't move in with another.

The plan: own the core, rent the overflow, keep the switch. Openly licensed models run on machines the community owns, connected over Longmont's own fiber. One gateway meters every request, enforces the cooperative's rules, and can send overflow to whichever host is serving the same open models best that week.

  • Shared machines get more done. Serving many people at once lets one machine produce many times the output it would for a single user. That's the math that makes a town-sized utility work.
  • Open models are close behind. Independent tracking puts the best open models a matter of months behind the best closed ones, plenty for summarizing a meeting, searching records or drafting a grant letter.
  • Sensitive records stay home. City work runs on separate, locked-down hardware that never touches the public service.
  • Library card sign-in. Residents get an allowance, and the civic apps here become the first users on day one.
  • Neighbors can share capacity. Members can lend spare machines overnight, and town utilities can link up, settling accounts the way libraries handle interlibrary loans.
